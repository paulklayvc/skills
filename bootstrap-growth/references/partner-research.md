# Partner research and qualification

Read this when a full plan includes finding customers, referral partners, influencers, or ambassadors, or when the user asks for a specific shortlist.

## Turn the offer into a search brief

Use the agreed industry, geography, language, customer problem, and partnership model. Decide what evidence would make a candidate useful for this pilot:

- **Founding customer:** a public workflow or business need the product can address, a plausible buying role, and room for the team to deliver the promised scope. Public signals establish a hypothesis, not the company's private budget or willingness to buy.
- **Referral partner or core influencer:** access to the right customers, relevant expertise or content, credible distribution, and a reason the proposed exchange would help their business or audience.
- **Ambassador:** evidence of genuine product use or relevant community participation, useful feedback, and sustainable access or referral benefits. Mark product affinity as unknown when it cannot be established.

Search for the selected profiles rather than every partnership type. Treat directories, search snippets, and suggested names as discovery leads; verify the claims used to qualify a candidate on the underlying pages.

## Verify what matters

Read primary sources where available: company sites, service pages, customer cases, partner directories, public profiles, creator content, and community or event pages. Check recent activity and publication dates when recency affects fit. Use secondary sources to supplement, not to manufacture certainty.

For each promising candidate, establish:

- What they do, whom they serve, and how that overlaps the target customer or problem.
- A concrete contribution they could make, supported by public evidence.
- Why the proposed benefits might matter to them; label this as a hypothesis until confirmed.
- A public business contact route, relevant introduction opportunity, or legitimate community entry point.
- The main mismatch or unknown that could prevent a deal.

Attach direct source links to the claims they support. Record the research date and distinguish published metrics from estimates. Do not infer audience engagement from follower counts alone, fabricate testimonials, or treat a public partner program as acceptance of this proposal. Use publicly offered business contact channels; do not investigate private identities or groups.

## Rank and connect to action

Build a focused shortlist, typically 5–10 candidates for a full plan, adjusted to the pilot's capacity and the available evidence. Fewer verified, fitting candidates are better than filling a quota. Rank by audience/problem fit, evidence of a useful contribution, feasibility of the exchange, and the route to a conversation. Explain the tradeoffs rather than assign precise scores unsupported by evidence.

Use a compact table with: candidate and role; evidence of fit with sources; potential contribution; tailored offer; contact or introduction route; main unknown; priority. State why the first targets should come first. Keep unknown audience size, pricing, referral policies, and willingness visibly unknown.

Draft a first-touch message for the highest-priority targets, connecting a verified detail about them to the user's confirmed value proposition. Use placeholders for unconfirmed customer results. A draft may propose terms; it must not imply an agreed partnership, existing relationship, or endorsement.

Map the first targets into the pilot's invitation and qualification stages. Include time spent researching and approaching them in the acquisition effort. Stop researching when there is enough evidence to choose the first cohort; expand only to resolve a material gap or replace rejected candidates. Research and drafts do not authorize sending messages or making commitments.
